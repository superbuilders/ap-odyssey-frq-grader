AP Odyssey · engine evaluation · 30 Oct 2026

Can we grade AP Lang essays automatically?

We ran four graders against all 54 essays College Board has published with official scores. Short answer: not well enough to put a grade on, but well enough to coach with — and the cheapest option is as good as the most expensive at the thing coaching actually needs.

What we were testing

AP Odyssey's roadmap has an FRQ grader on it: a student writes a free-response essay, gets scored against College Board's rubric, and is told how to earn the next point. The open question was whether any model can do that reliably enough to show a student.

Every AP Lang essay is scored out of 6, in three rows: Thesis (0–1), Evidence & Commentary (0–4), and Sophistication (0–1). Those three numbers are the whole task.

Why BaconWrite is in this

Most of you haven't touched it, so: BaconWrite is the HS-Writing-Course repo — a separate superbuilders project building a structured high-school writing course. It's a Next.js app with a production AI grader that scores student writing against rubrics. It isn't AP-specific, but it's the closest working thing to what we're proposing, and it matters here for three reasons.

They already evaluated this exact model. In September they ran Jev against their Claude grader on 101 submissions and found Claude clearly better — 95.0% against 86.1%, statistically solid. Read only that, and you'd conclude Jev is the wrong tool and stop.

But their test used synthetic inputs. Their gold labels were author-assigned intents on AI-written practice responses, not scores from human raters. Their own writeup says so, and ends by naming the test that would actually settle it — real human-scored essays — as blocked on a data-sharing approval that hadn't come through.

And they have an AP panel already measured against real AP readers. A judge panel scoring the AP three-row rubric, agreement 0.672 at n=18, with a confidence interval of [0.318, 0.836] — wide enough to be compatible with almost anything.

College Board's published sample essays are public documents with no student data in them. So the test BaconWrite was blocked on, we could just run. We used their panel as one of our four graders.

The problem with that table

College Board labels its three samples per question A, B and C — and A is always the best one, in all 18 question sets we pulled. So a ten-line script that reads nothing but that letter beats every real grader.

No grader saw the letter; we checked all four code paths. They just can't beat a cheat sheet they never read. This corpus can't settle how accurate anyone is. Every absolute number above has to be read next to that red row.

What survives anyway

Nobody is close to a grade of record

The best grader agreed with College Board on all three rows for 24 of 54 essays. For a score that goes on a student's record, the exact number has to be right, and none of these are near that. This isn't marginal — it's settled.

"Claude beats Jev" does not replicate

BaconWrite found that gap at a significance of p=0.004 on their synthetic set. On real human-scored essays it's p=0.136 — well inside noise. The expensive panel leads on every point estimate and trails on none, but at this sample size the $0.22 grader and the $0.0005 grader are not distinguishable.

They are all equally good at ranking

Given two essays, which is stronger? Every grader lands at 82–83%, and every pairwise test between them is non-significant. That is the capability a coach needs — "this draft is stronger than your last, and here's where" — and the half-second classifier delivers it as well as the fifteen-second Opus panel.

Jev has one specific, fixable flaw

It never awards a 4. Not once in 54 essays, 16 of which earned one. But its underlying score separates those 16 from the rest by a clear margin — it sees them and won't say so. That is a scale that needs rebasing, not a model that cannot read. We fixed it; the section below is what that cost.

The models had not memorized the essays

Worth ruling out, since these PDFs have been public for years. Three probes: zero shared eight-word runs across 30 continuation attempts, zero self-reported recall across 108 asks, and below-chance accuracy at naming which year an essay came from. Clean.

Two things we got wrong

Our BaconWrite "production grader" arm was void, and that was our fault. It scored 8 of 54, which looked like a damning result for their system. It wasn't. Our adapter modelled the whole 0–4 evidence row as a single pass/fail criterion, so it could only ever emit 0 or 4. Their grader does proportional partial credit across weighted criteria — we just never gave it more than one criterion to weigh. The arm is dropped. No finding here reflects badly on BaconWrite's grader; their AP panel was our best performer.

Jev is 18× faster than the panel, not 64×. The 64× figure came from BaconWrite's writeup on a different task and we repeated it before measuring. The cost advantage is real and large but derived from token counts against a published rate, not recorded directly.

Fixing Jev's calibration

Jev's 24 of 54 understated it, and we could say exactly why. Its signal was trapped in [1.24, 3.38], so its own decode never emitted a 4. Sixteen of the 54 essays are 4s, which means 16 were marked wrong before the grader read a word. Its predictions came out {1:1, 2:26, 3:27, 4:0} against a gold spread of {1:8, 2:13, 3:17, 4:16}; 23 of its 30 errors sat at the two ends of the scale.

That is a range problem, not a judgment problem. The obvious fix is to ask better — so we tried that first, on all 54 essays, live. It failed completely, and the failure is the most useful result in this section.

Four ways of asking, none of which moved it

We gave Jev, in turn, two officially-scored essays as worked anchors; a flat statement of the real score distribution including the sentence “Level 4 is a common outcome, not a rare one”; a decomposition that bypasses the score head entirely and asks four yes/no questions (is this at least a 1? a 2? a 3? a 4?) composed in code; and a head-to-head comparison against a known level-3 anchor. 324 calls, no errors, about eight cents.

The control settles it: re-running the identical request reproduces the ceiling to within 0.01. It is not noise, and no amount of better prompting touched it — anchors moved the top of the range by −0.01, the stated distribution by −0.03. The ladder was the most promising variant and the most carefully built, and it is internally coherent — monotone on all 54 essays, which is a real result about Jev's consistency. It also scores 22, below the baseline it was meant to beat. Its “at least a 4?” probability never exceeds 0.670 anywhere in the corpus.

So Jev's reluctance at the top of the scale is not an information deficit. It is a property of the calibrated heads, and it shows up in the yes/no head exactly as it does in the score head. You cannot ask your way out of it — which means the honest fix is to leave Jev's question alone and move the grade boundaries afterwards.

Moving the boundaries instead

A monotone rescaling of the signal Jev already produced. No new calls. We fitted five mappings and scored each two ways — leave-one-out, and a year-holdout that fits on two exam years and scores the third, rotated. Only the second tests whether a mapping survives a new prompt set, a new rubric and the 2023/24 transcription boundary.

The headline hides three things, and all of them matter more than the headline.

The gain is bought from the middle of the scale, and the bill is larger than it first looked. Our first pass reported the middle using predictions refitted on each fold. The module we would actually ship uses three frozen thresholds, and on those the damage is worse. Per gold level, before and after:

Level 2 falls from 92% correct to 38%. Net, the shipped mapping is +3 essays, not +7 — nearly everything it gains at the top it hands back in the middle. Telling a 2 from a 3 is the most common real distinction in AP Lang scoring, and at that job this calibration is a regression. That is a genuine split by use case, not a footnote: if you need the middle, keep the uncalibrated head. Adjacent agreement moved the same way — 52 of 54 within one point after, 53 of 54 before.

Most individual scores are coin flips. Jev's evidence signal drifts up to 0.18 between identical runs, and the usable part of its range is about one point wide, so the three thresholds sit close together. 37 of 54 essays land within 0.18 of one. Re-running the whole study with that drift injected, quantile matching holds at 30.2 ± 2.1 — but the grid-searched cut points, which looked better on the same data, fall to 27.3 ± 2.1 and have about a one-in-ten chance of beating no calibration at all. That is why we chose the mapping that scores lower in-sample: its thresholds do not sit wedged between adjacent training points.

The run-to-run noise is bigger than the improvement. Fit the same mapping, then score it on the archived signal and on a fresh re-run of the byte-identical request: 27 and 32. A five-point swing from nothing but drift — larger than the entire gain we are claiming over the uncalibrated baseline. If Row B accuracy feeds a decision, average three reads of the signal before mapping; rank-averaging three reads held 32 with visibly less variance.

What this does and does not change

Calibration is worth doing — it is free, it costs no latency, and it removes a defect that was costing Jev 16 essays before it read a word. It does not change the conclusion, and it is weaker than the headline suggests. The year-holdout says 31; leave-one-out says 27; the thresholds we would actually freeze and ship deliver 29–30 on a fresh run. With a standard error near ±3.7 essays, those are one number.

So is the comparison everyone asks about. We could not run McNemar against the Opus panel — only its 33/54 summary survives, not its per-essay calls — so we bounded it instead. On 54 essays, a two-essay gap gives p = 0.500 even if every disagreement falls the same way. You need a gap near nine essays before any arrangement of this corpus reaches significance. Which means the panel was never significantly ahead of uncalibrated Jev either, at p = 0.136. The right reading is not “Jev still loses.” It is that 24, 31 and 33 are three numbers this corpus cannot tell apart, and the gap that justified preferring the expensive panel was never established. Calibration moved Jev from looks-worse to indistinguishable. That changes what you may claim, not what was measured.

What we'd do next

  • Use Jev for the student-facing coach. It ranks as well as anything we tested, returns in under a second, and costs essentially nothing. Ship it uncalibrated for coaching: the raw head errs low rather than high, and a coach that under-rates never tells a student a weak draft is excellent. Calibration voids that — the rescaled grader predicts 16–18 level-4s where there are 16, and runs slightly hot on a fresh pass. Reach for the calibrated thresholds when you need the top of the scale across a batch, not when you are talking to one student about one draft.
  • Don't ship any of these as a grade. Including the expensive ones.
  • Change what Jev is asked, not where it is cut. Calibration is done and it bought a better ruler, not a better reader — the mapping is monotone, so Jev ranks all 54 essays in exactly the order it did before. The open move is to pre-segment the essay in code and ask one findable thing per unit (does this claim carry evidence? does this span explain how?), leaving every count to arithmetic. The honest caveat: the nearest thing we tested, the yes/no ladder, scored below baseline — though it still asked for a holistic level four times rather than asking about one claim at a time. Measure it by whether Jev's rows become less correlated with each other: ours sit at +0.807 where College Board's real rows sit at +0.558.
  • Build the 2026 corpus with the letters scrambled. The prompts and rubric are already downloaded; College Board hasn't published 2026 samples yet. A set where the filename doesn't leak the rank is the only way to get a clean accuracy number — and it's after every model's training cutoff, which closes the last open question from the contamination probe.
  • Test the hybrid. Run Jev, escalate to the panel only where Jev lands at its ceiling. That halves cost and time and sits between the two on accuracy, though not yet distinguishably from either.
